## Why AI Inference Costs Require Lifecycle-Level Governance

P11 (Economics) of the Agentic Engineering Manifesto governs whether a specific loop iteration is cost-justified relative to its outcome. It asks, for a given loop execution: did the tokens consumed and the inference cost incurred produce an output whose value — measured against the loop's specification — justifies the expenditure? That question is necessary and non-negotiable at the inner loop level. It is not sufficient at the organisational level.

FinOps governance at the lifecycle level governs the portfolio of loop iterations: whether the organisation knows what it is spending on AI inference in aggregate, whether costs are attributed to the teams and products that incur them, whether budgets exist before costs are committed, and whether cost trends are visible before they become budget crises. An organisation can satisfy P11 for every individual loop iteration and still find itself at the end of a quarter with an inference bill it cannot explain, cannot attribute, and could not have forecast from any governance artefact it produced.

AI inference costs are structurally different from traditional compute costs in ways that make portfolio-level accumulation particularly difficult to detect without deliberate governance. Traditional compute costs are capacity-bound: you provision a server or a container fleet at a known rate and the bill is roughly proportional to provisioned capacity over time. Inference costs are consumption-bound: they scale with the number of input tokens, the number of output tokens, and the model tier used for each call, and they scale non-linearly with context length as context windows expand. A loop iteration that processes a large code repository in context may cost an order of magnitude more than one that processes a narrow domain document, even if both produce outputs of comparable business value. That variance is invisible to a governance framework that measures only whether each individual iteration was cost-justified.

An organisation running multiple agentic systems simultaneously — development loops in several product teams, evaluation pipelines in CI, inference serving in multiple production systems — can accumulate significant inference spend before any individual system triggers a cost anomaly alert. The anomaly detection problem for portfolios is different from the anomaly detection problem for individual systems: a system whose cost is within its expected range may still be contributing to a portfolio-level accumulation that is unsustainable. Portfolio-level cost governance is the mechanism for detecting that accumulation.

The "Economical" condition in the Agentic DoD governs whether a single loop output was cost-justified at the time of production. It cannot detect whether the portfolio of outputs over a quarter was cost-justified relative to the portfolio's value delivery. That requires cost attribution across systems and teams, unit economics that make cost rates comparable across products, budgets that were set before costs were committed, and quarterly reporting that connects cost trends to value delivery. These are finance and governance processes, not inner-loop conditions — they operate at a timescale and level of aggregation that the inner loop cannot govern.

Without cost attribution, teams cannot be held accountable for the cost of their loop iterations. Without accountability, the organisation cannot compare cost efficiency across products or teams, cannot identify which systems are consuming disproportionate inference budget relative to their value delivery, and cannot make informed prioritisation decisions about which systems to maintain, optimise, or retire. Cost attribution is not an accounting formality — it is the precondition for any economically rational portfolio management of agentic systems.

## Cost Attribution and Tagging Standards

Every agentic workload — loop iterations in development, evaluation runs in CI, inference in production — must be instrumented for cost attribution before it runs, not retrospectively. The principle that governance must be encoded in the specification before execution, not appended after, applies to cost governance as directly as it applies to security or domain boundary constraints. A workload that runs without attribution tags has incurred cost that cannot be assigned to the system, team, environment, or business purpose that incurred it. Retroactively assigning attribution tags to untagged historical spend is possible but unreliable: the tags will be estimates, and estimates compound into budget misalignments that grow with the age of the untagged data.

Attribution tags are a specification-level constraint. Before any agentic workload is deployed — at any environment level, including development — the specification must define the attribution tags that apply to its inference calls. The steward is accountable for confirming that tags are defined and correctly applied before the workload runs.

The following tagging dimensions are required for all agentic workloads:

1. **System identifier.** Which production system or development project incurred the cost. This is the primary attribution dimension for production systems and the reference dimension for correlating development costs with the system they are building toward.

2. **Team or product owner.** Which team is accountable for the spend. In a multi-team organisation, this dimension enables team-level cost reporting and team-level accountability for inference efficiency.

3. **Environment.** Which environment — development, test, integration, staging, or production — incurred the cost. Development costs must be separated from production costs; conflating them distorts both the cost-per-outcome metric (which measures production efficiency) and the production cost forecast (which should be derived from production data alone). Development inference costs are governed by the team's token budget discipline; production inference costs are governed by the operational DoD and the production cost baseline.

4. **Phase.** Which maturity phase the work corresponds to. This dimension is used to correlate cost efficiency with governance maturity and to identify efficiency changes as teams progress through phases — a team advancing from Phase 2 to Phase 3 should show measurable improvement in cost-per-outcome as specification discipline tightens.

5. **Blast radius tier.** The governance tier of the work. Higher-tier work typically warrants higher cost scrutiny: a Tier 3 specification with autonomous production-facing execution has a higher cost profile and a higher cost anomaly sensitivity than a Tier 1 internal-only specification.

6. **Business cost centre.** The organisational unit accountable for the budget. This dimension connects inference spend to the finance function's reporting hierarchy and enables chargeback or showback to the business units that sponsor agentic development.

The FOCUS (FinOps Open Cost and Usage Specification) is the vendor-neutral standard for representing cost and usage data in a normalised format comparable across providers and tooling choices. Attribution tags defined in FOCUS-compatible formats are portable across provider changes, ensuring that cost governance data does not need to be rebuilt if the organisation changes inference providers. Organisations adopting the ASDLC's cost attribution standards should confirm that their tagging schema is expressible in FOCUS-compatible formats before finalising the specification. This is a one-time design decision with significant long-term portability value; retroactively restructuring attribution data to conform to FOCUS after years of accumulation is substantially more expensive than designing for compatibility from the outset.

The requirement for pre-run attribution tagging does not apply only to production workloads. Development environment inference — including exploratory loop iterations that precede a formal specification, evaluation runs in CI pipelines, and test environment inference — must carry attribution tags calibrated to development granularity. Development tags may use team-level rather than system-level granularity where no specific system identifier exists yet, but the environment tag must correctly classify the work as development rather than production regardless of the granularity available for other dimensions.

## Unit Economics

Unit economics make the cost dimension of P11 legible at the portfolio level. Without unit economics, cost governance operates on totals and trends: spend went up, spend went down, spend is within or outside budget. With unit economics, it operates on rates: is the cost-per-outcome improving, stable, or degrading over time? Rates are comparable across products, across teams, and across time periods in ways that totals are not. An organisation that spends twice as much on inference as it did last year but delivers four times as many governed outcomes is improving. An organisation that maintains flat inference spend while value delivery declines is degrading. Neither of these facts is visible from totals alone.

Four unit economics metrics are required at the Walk maturity level (Phase 3 and above).

**Cost per loop iteration.** Total inference cost — input tokens, output tokens, and any other consumption-based costs incurred within the governed loop execution boundary — from specification entry to governed loop output, per iteration. This is the primary efficiency metric for the engineering execution layer. A cost per loop iteration that is growing without a corresponding improvement in loop output quality or business value realisation is an efficiency degradation signal: the loop is spending more to produce outputs of equivalent or declining quality. This signal warrants a specification analysis to determine whether the context management approach, model tier routing, or reasoning depth governance is operating as specified.

**Cost per business outcome.** Total delivery and operational cost divided by the number of business outcomes delivered, where business outcomes are defined by the Layer 1 success criterion for the relevant initiative. This is the primary efficiency metric for the full lifecycle. It captures the relationship between all costs — inference, human labour, infrastructure, and operational overhead — and the value delivered in business terms. An organisation where the cost per loop iteration is stable but the cost per business outcome is growing is producing technically efficient loop outputs that are not translating into business value. That is a demand layer quality signal: either the success criteria are being set incorrectly, the value measurement infrastructure is not capturing actual outcomes, or the demand layer is funding work that does not reach value realisation.

**Production inference cost rate.** Total inference cost for a production system per unit of production output, calibrated to the system's output type: per request, per transaction, per decision, or per defined time period. This is the primary production cost health signal. A production inference cost rate that is growing without a corresponding increase in output volume is a quality degradation or configuration failure signal. The system is spending more tokens per output, which typically indicates that context is expanding without governance, that reasoning depth is increasing beyond what the specification intended, or that model routing is directing requests to higher-cost tiers without a corresponding quality justification. Each of these is a distinct root cause requiring a distinct response; the signal identifies that investigation is warranted, not which specific cause applies.

**Inference cost as a percentage of total delivery cost.** Inference cost divided by total delivery cost including human labour, infrastructure, and operational overhead. This metric tracks the proportion of total spend driven by AI inference, which is relevant for financial planning at the portfolio level. As organisations scale agentic delivery, the inference cost proportion typically grows as human labour in the loop is reduced and agent execution volume increases. That growth must be anticipated in budgets; an organisation that budgets for total delivery cost using prior-period proportions without adjusting for the shift toward inference spend will systematically underestimate inference costs and overestimate labour costs.

Warning thresholds requiring steward investigation: a production inference cost rate growing faster than output volume over any rolling 90-day window is an efficiency degradation signal. Inference cost as a percentage of total delivery cost exceeding 40% for a Tier 1 system is a governance signal — at that proportion, a Tier 1 system is exhibiting an inference cost profile more consistent with Tier 2 or Tier 3 complexity, which may indicate that the blast radius tier assessment was set too low, that the specification is not adequately constraining reasoning depth, or that model tier routing is not operating within its intended parameters.

## Token Budget Governance

Token budgets are the mechanism by which P11 economics governance is implemented as an enforceable constraint rather than a post-hoc observation. A token budget transforms "this iteration should be cost-justified" from a principle into a control: the agent cannot spend more than the budget permits, and any attempt to do so produces a governed response rather than an unconstrained escalation of cost.

A token budget is a specification-level hard constraint defining the maximum tokens — input plus output plus context — that the agent may consume in a single loop iteration. The token budget is set before the loop runs, based on three inputs: the cost model for the model tier being used, the complexity of the specification as assessed by the specification analyst, and the organisation's cost threshold for the blast radius tier of the work. Tier 1 specifications carry lower cost thresholds than Tier 3 specifications; this differential is intentional — higher blast radius work is expected to require more thorough reasoning, and higher cost thresholds reflect that expectation, but they also create higher cost accountability obligations.

Token budget enforcement is a runtime control, not a post-hoc measurement. An agent approaching the budget ceiling must either complete the work with degraded but safe output — where safe degradation is defined in the specification as an explicit contingency, not left as an implicit behaviour — or abort and escalate to a human for resolution. An agent that exceeds its token budget without escalation has violated a governance constraint in the same category as exceeding its domain boundary or attempting a prohibited action. It is not a cost efficiency failure; it is a governance failure. The appropriate incident classification for a budget overrun without escalation is a governance breach, not a cost anomaly.

Token budget calibration is iterative. The initial token budget for a specification is an estimate based on the assessment inputs above. After the first loop iteration using the specification, actual token consumption is compared to the budget. Consistent consumption below 50% of the budget indicates that the budget was set too generously; an excessively generous budget provides no governance constraint and produces misleading cost projections. Consistent consumption approaching or exceeding the budget on multiple iterations indicates either that the budget was set too low or that the specification requires decomposition into smaller loop iterations with individual budgets. The default response to budget pressure is specification refinement — decomposing the specification or tightening the scope of the loop iteration — not budget expansion. Budget expansion without specification review is a governance control weakening, not a calibration.

Token budget review cadence: token budgets for active specifications are reviewed quarterly as part of the demand layer governance cycle. Budgets set significantly above actual usage — defined as average actual consumption below 40% of the budget — for two consecutive quarters should be tightened to a level that provides genuine governance constraint while still accommodating observed variation. Budgets that are frequently triggering escalation require specification analysis within the quarter: is the work genuinely larger than the budget permits, or is the agent reasoning beyond the depth the specification intended? These are different problems with different responses. The first warrants a budget revision following decomposition review. The second warrants a specification update to constrain reasoning depth before any budget adjustment is considered.

## Inference Cost Budgeting

Traditional compute budget models — provisioned capacity with predictable unit costs — do not transfer directly to AI inference cost forecasting. Inference costs depend on input and output token distributions, which vary with real-world inputs in ways that are difficult to predict from synthetic test cases. A development-phase cost projection based on structured test inputs may significantly underestimate production inference costs when real-world inputs are longer, more variable, or more reasoning-intensive than the test set anticipated. This structural forecasting challenge requires a budgeting approach that distinguishes between pre-deployment estimates and post-deployment baselines, and that requires the latter to replace the former before committed production budgets are finalised.

**Demand layer cost-benefit assessment.** For Tier 2 and Tier 3 specifications, a projected inference cost range must be documented as part of the build versus buy assessment, which forms Step 0 of the Demand-to-Specification Bridge in Layer 1 (see Demand & Value). The projection covers three components: expected loop iterations — the number of development and evaluation loop iterations expected to produce a deployable output; expected per-iteration cost — the token budget for the specification multiplied by the applicable model tier rate, producing a per-iteration cost estimate; and expected production inference cost — estimated token consumption per production request multiplied by the anticipated request volume, producing a monthly production cost estimate. The projection is not a guarantee. It is a planning basis that the product owner accepts as the cost dimension of the demand item's value proposition. If actual costs diverge materially from the projection before the loop completes — defined as actual costs exceeding the projection by more than 50% without a corresponding expansion of scope — the accountable human must be notified and the demand layer cost-benefit assessment updated before the loop continues.

**Production cost baseline.** Operational budgets for production inference must be set based on observed usage data from the first 30 days of production operation, not on pre-deployment estimates. The first 30 days under real production input distribution establish the production cost baseline: the inference cost rate per unit of production output at the volume and input characteristics of actual use. The steward is accountable for establishing this baseline and communicating it to the finance function for budget planning by the end of the 30-day window. Production inference cost budgets that are committed before the 30-day baseline period is complete are estimates; they must be formally revised once the baseline data is available, with the revised budget replacing the estimate in all financial planning documents.

**Cost forecast horizon.** Cost forecasts for active production systems should extend three to four quarters ahead, updated quarterly. The forecast draws on four inputs: historical production inference cost rate derived from the baseline and subsequent monitoring data; projected production volume growth based on the system's growth trajectory and planned capacity; anticipated model routing changes — planned updates to routing policy that would shift the cost profile — and their expected effective date; and scheduled specification updates that may alter the per-request token consumption profile. Cost forecasts that extend beyond four quarters without updating model tier pricing inputs are unreliable. The commercial environment for AI inference is not stable over multi-year horizons; pricing changes, new model tiers, and routing efficiency improvements make extended-horizon forecasts materially less valuable than quarterly re-forecasting against current pricing and recent usage data.

## FinOps Maturity Levels (Phase-Calibrated)

FinOps governance, like engineering governance, matures incrementally. Attempting to implement Run-level cost optimisation before the organisation has cost visibility is not accelerated maturity — it is wasted governance effort applied to a foundation that does not yet exist. The maturity levels below correspond to the ASDLC inner loop phases and align with the FinOps Foundation Framework's Crawl, Walk, and Run maturity model.

**Crawl (Phase 1–2): Cost Visibility.** Inference costs are measurable and visible at the organisation level. Attribution to individual teams, systems, or business outcomes is not yet required. Unit economics are not yet calculated. Token budgets are defined as a best effort but are not enforced as hard runtime constraints. The primary objective at this level is establishing that costs are instrumentable: the organisation can answer "how much did we spend on AI inference this month across the entire portfolio?" even if it cannot attribute that spend to specific systems or teams. Reaching this level requires inference call instrumentation to be present in all agentic workloads — without instrumentation, even organisation-level visibility is unavailable.

**Early Walk (Phase 3): Cost Attribution and Basic Governance.** Inference costs are attributed to individual systems and teams using the tagging standards defined in this document. Token budgets are defined and enforced as runtime constraints for all Tier 2 and above specifications. Cost per loop iteration and production inference cost rate are tracked as operational metrics. Cost anomaly alerts are configured for all production systems. The organisation can answer "which system and team incurred this cost, and is it within expected bounds?" Chargeback or showback to teams is in a pilot state for at least one system. The 30-day production cost baseline process is in operation for new production deployments.

**Mature Walk (Phase 4): Unit Economics and Portfolio Governance.** All four unit economics metrics are tracked and reported quarterly at the portfolio level. Cost-benefit assessments are required as part of the demand layer process for all Tier 2 and Tier 3 demand items. Production cost baselines are established within 30 days of deployment for all new systems without exception. Cost forecasts extend three to four quarters for all active production systems and are updated quarterly. Chargeback or showback to business units is operational across the production fleet — business units can inspect the inference costs attributable to their systems. The FinOps review in the Govern phase of the inner loop includes a comparison of projected versus actual costs for the completed loop iteration, with any variance above 25% triggering a documented explanation.

**Run (Phase 5): Continuous Optimisation.** Cost optimisation patterns (see the following section) are evaluated systematically across the portfolio on a defined review cadence, not only at the point of specification. Committed usage arrangements are reviewed and renegotiated annually for systems where usage is predictable and volume justifies commitment. The economics owner produces a quarterly portfolio cost performance report for finance and engineering leadership covering all four unit economics metrics, trend analysis, anomaly summary, and optimisation actions taken or planned. Cost efficiency — measured as cost per business outcome — is a formal engineering platform KPI reviewed in the same forum as output quality rate and escalation frequency. The organisation can answer "what is our aggregate cost per business outcome across the portfolio, and is it improving quarter over quarter?"

## Cost Optimisation Patterns

Cost optimisation patterns are specification-level and architectural decisions, not operational settings that can be toggled on after the fact. They are addressed at the point where they are most effective: the inner loop where they become specification constraints, the demand layer where cost-benefit assessment incorporates them, and production operations where they are monitored for continued effectiveness. A cost optimisation pattern that is applied as an operational workaround without a corresponding specification update has not been governed — it has been patched, and it will be overwritten by the next specification-driven deployment.

**Prompt caching.** Where a specification requires repeated processing of the same context prefix across multiple requests — a long system prompt, a large reference document, a fixed few-shot example set, a domain knowledge base — caching that context across requests reduces input token consumption. The specification should identify cacheable context components as a constraint at design time, not leave caching as an ad-hoc implementation decision for the engineer executing the loop. A specification that does not address caching for a workload with a stable, repeated context is leaving a significant cost optimisation unaddressed.

**Request batching.** Where the production workload permits combining multiple independent requests into a single inference call, batching reduces per-call overhead and can improve throughput efficiency. Batching introduces latency: the system must wait to accumulate a batch before any individual request in the batch is processed. The specification must define the acceptable latency tolerance for the use case before batching can be responsibly applied — applying batching to a workload that requires sub-second response introduces quality degradation in the form of latency that the cost saving does not justify. The latency tolerance is a demand layer constraint, defined by the business need, not a default assumption.

**Model tier routing.** Not every inference request requires the largest, most capable model tier. A routing policy that directs simpler, well-defined tasks — classification, extraction, structured formatting, templated generation — to smaller and less expensive model tiers while reserving larger tiers for extended reasoning and novel problem-solving reduces inference cost without quality degradation, provided the routing thresholds are calibrated against actual task complexity. Model tier routing is the cost dimension of P11 in the manifesto; the routing policy is a specification-level governance decision, not an operational parameter. A routing policy that drifts from its specification — routing more requests to higher-cost tiers without a corresponding quality requirement — is a cost anomaly with a specification root cause.

**Retrieval augmentation in place of extended context.** Expanding the context window to include large documents or broad knowledge sets increases token consumption non-linearly: many model architectures exhibit quadratic or near-quadratic attention cost scaling with context length, and even architectures that avoid this in principle still incur the input token cost of the expanded context. Where the relevant information from a large document set can be retrieved with acceptable precision using a retrieval approach, querying for the relevant sections rather than including the full document set in every request is a structural cost control that scales with the volume of available information rather than against it. The specification should define the retrieval approach as a design constraint where the knowledge set is large, not default to full-context inclusion for simplicity.

**Response length governance.** Output token consumption scales with the agent's response length. Unbounded output generation is not a neutral choice — it is a decision to allow inference costs to scale with the agent's verbosity rather than with the information content required by the task. Specifications should define output length constraints for all structured outputs — classification results, extraction outputs, formatted documents, code artefacts — where the expected output has bounded length. An agent that produces longer outputs than the specification requires is not more thorough; it is over-generating relative to the governance constraint. The response to consistent over-generation is a specification update defining the output length constraint, not an acceptance that the agent reasons verbosely.

## Governance Roles

**Economics owner.** A named role at the portfolio level — organisationally separate from any individual delivery team — responsible for monitoring aggregate inference costs across the production fleet, setting and reviewing cost thresholds at the portfolio level, producing quarterly cost performance reports for finance and engineering leadership, and escalating anomalies that span multiple systems. Systemic cost inefficiencies — a shared infrastructure component that is over-provisioned across all teams that use it — are economics owner escalations. Provider pricing changes that affect the portfolio cost model require an economics owner response: updating forecasts, assessing commitment arrangements, and communicating the impact to affected stewards. Model routing policy drift that is producing portfolio-level cost growth rather than system-level anomalies requires an economics owner investigation rather than a system-level steward response. The economics owner does not govern individual system costs; that is the system steward's accountability. The economics owner governs the portfolio visibility and reporting function that makes individual cost accountability meaningful in aggregate.

**System steward (cost dimension).** The system steward is accountable for the production cost baseline, cost anomaly investigation, and the quarterly cost efficiency review for their system. The steward's cost accountability is not separate from their other stewardship responsibilities — it is expressed through the operational DoD review cycle in the same way that security scan compliance, trace retention, and escalation chain currency are expressed. Cost conditions that fail the operational DoD — cost monitoring not configured, cost anomaly alerts not active, production cost baseline not established within 30 days of deployment — are stewardship failures with the same standing as runbook staleness or security scan lapse. A steward who has cost monitoring configured but has not reviewed the cost trend in the quarterly review cycle has not met the cost accountability condition, regardless of whether the system is within budget.

**Product owner (demand cost accountability).** The product owner is accountable for the cost-benefit assessment at the demand layer. This accountability has two components: ensuring that the projected inference cost is included in the Tier 2 and Tier 3 demand item assessment before the specification enters the engineering loop, and ensuring that the actual cost post-deployment is compared against the projection as part of the value realisation measurement that Layer 1 requires. A product owner who tracks business outcome realisation without tracking cost outcome realisation is measuring value delivery incompletely. Cost outcome — did the system deliver its value at or below the projected cost? — is the economic dimension of the value realisation measurement, and it is the product owner's accountability, not the steward's.

## Cloud Waste Detection

Inference infrastructure that is provisioned but not actively serving production workloads is waste: it incurs cost and provides no value. Agentic systems are susceptible to specific waste patterns that require active steward attention and cannot be detected through normal cost anomaly alerting alone, because the costs are within expected ranges — the problem is that the costs are not producing corresponding value.

**Idle inference endpoints.** Provisioned inference capacity that has not received requests within a defined window should be flagged for review. The threshold for this review is 72 consecutive hours of zero production traffic during a period when production traffic would reasonably be expected, based on the system's historical traffic pattern. The steward's response is either to decommission the idle endpoint — following the retirement governance procedures in the maintenance lifecycle — or to document why the endpoint is being maintained in standby state, including the business justification for the standby cost and the review date for reassessment. Undocumented idle endpoints that are not flagged for decommissioning are operational DoD failures in the cost dimension.

**Development environment bleed.** Development and staging environment inference costs that are incurred at the same rate as production but are not subject to the same cost governance represent a common and systematically underestimated waste category. Development costs must be separated from production costs in attribution tagging — this is a tagging standard requirement, not an optional practice — and development teams must be made aware of their inference spend so that token budget discipline can be applied to exploratory work, not only to governed loop iterations. A development team that has no visibility into its inference costs will not apply cost discipline to exploratory iterations; the economics owner is accountable for ensuring that development cost visibility is available to all active development teams at the Early Walk maturity level and above.

**Oversized context windows.** Where trace data shows that the agent's context window is consistently much larger than the tokens the agent actually references in its reasoning — a pattern detectable through reasoning trace analysis, where the agent cites or engages with only a fraction of the context it received — the context window is oversized for the actual task. Oversized context windows are a form of inference waste: tokens are billed for context that the agent does not use. The specification's context management approach should be revisited when this pattern is observed consistently over multiple loop iterations. The appropriate response is a specification update to the context management constraint, not a cost absorption. An oversized context window that is never addressed by a specification update is a recurring waste that compounds over the lifetime of the system.

## Connections to Other ASDLC Layers

FinOps governance is cross-cutting rather than layer-specific because the cost of agentic delivery is incurred at every layer of the ASDLC. The following maps the FinOps governance requirements defined in this document to their corresponding points in the layer structure.

**Layer 1 (Demand & Value).** Cost-benefit assessment is required for Tier 2 and Tier 3 demand items as part of the Demand-to-Specification Bridge. The token budget is defined as a specification constraint at this layer before the specification enters the engineering loop. The build versus buy assessment includes an inference cost comparison. The product owner carries accountability for projected cost and post-deployment cost outcome realisation.

**Layer 2 (Engineering Execution).** P11 governs per-loop cost and quality tradeoffs at the inner loop level. The token budget defined at Layer 1 is enforced as a runtime constraint throughout engineering execution. Cost optimisation patterns are expressed as specification constraints at design time, not applied as operational adjustments after deployment. The Govern phase of the inner loop includes a cost review comparing projected versus actual costs for the completed iteration, with variances above 25% requiring a documented explanation in the evidence bundle.

**Layer 3 (Release).** The evidence bundle includes cost projection versus actual for the loop iteration. Release governance confirms that production cost monitoring is configured and that cost anomaly alerting is active before release is authorised. A release gate condition for Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems is that the steward has confirmed attribution tagging is correctly implemented and verified in the deployment artefact.

**Layer 4 (Operations).** The operational DoD requires cost anomaly alerting to be configured and active. The steward is accountable for establishing the production cost baseline within 30 days of deployment. The quarterly operational DoD review includes cost performance assessment against the unit economics metrics. Maintenance governance covers provider dependency cost risk — assessment of how provider pricing changes or model deprecation would affect the system's cost profile — and the response plan for material cost increases arising from provider-side changes.

---

*FinOps governance is not a financial control separate from engineering governance — it is the economic accountability layer that makes engineering governance legible to the organisation. Without it, the Agentic Engineering Manifesto governs quality and safety in a cost vacuum. With it, every loop iteration, every deployment, and every production system operates within a financial accountability structure that connects engineering decisions to organisational economics.*
